Case math is not usually what decides an interview. What it does is take up
room. If you are spending working memory on 4,200 x 0.35, you are not spending
it on whether that is the right number to compute, and the second kind of
mistake is the one interviewers remember.

So the target is not speed for its own sake. It is making the arithmetic cheap
enough that you can keep thinking while you do it.

## Round first, then tighten

Most candidates try to be exact from the first step. Do the opposite. Get a
rough answer out loud, then refine it.

For 4,200 x 0.35: a third of 4,200 is 1,400, so the answer sits a little above
that. Then 4,200 x 0.3 is 1,260 and 4,200 x 0.05 is 210, giving 1,470.

The interviewer has a usable number in a few seconds and an exact one shortly
after. In a client meeting the order works the same way.

## Learn the conversions that come up

Not times tables. These:

- Percentages as fractions. 33% is about 1/3, 25% is 1/4, 12.5% is 1/8, 17% is
  about 1/6. Dividing beats multiplying by a decimal almost every time.
- Growth rates. The rule of 72 covers anything a case will ask of you. Seven
  percent a year roughly doubles in a decade.
- Population anchors. The U.S. is around 340 million, Canada around 41 million,
  households roughly a third of population. Three or four of these save you from
  building a market size out of nothing.
- Per-person scaling. A dollar per person per year in the U.S. is a market of
  about $340 million. Scaling from that beats building up from scratch.

## Handle magnitude separately from digits

Write 4.2M, not 4,200,000. Do the digits, do the zeros, then put them back
together. Most case math errors are order-of-magnitude slips rather than digit
slips, and separating the two steps catches them.

## Narrate before you calculate

Say what you are about to do. "I will take fleet size, multiply by annual
maintenance cost per vehicle, then take the warranty-eligible share." If the
approach is wrong, the interviewer can stop you before you spend ninety seconds
on arithmetic in the wrong direction. It also buys you thinking time.

## How to practise

Twenty minutes a day beats two hours on a Sunday.

1. Timed drills for speed. Multiplication and percentages, ten minutes.
2. Estimation for judgment. Size something and commit to a number before
   checking: coffee sales at a campus, tires sold in Ontario, elevators in
   Toronto. Ten minutes.
3. Do it out loud. Silent practice builds a skill you cannot use in an
   interview.

Track your errors rather than your times. After a week they usually fall into
one or two categories, and fixing those does more for speed than more drilling.

## The bar

Lower than most candidates assume. Rounding sensibly, sanity-checking your own
answer and catching your own slip is most of what the interviewer is watching
for, and none of it requires you to be quick.
