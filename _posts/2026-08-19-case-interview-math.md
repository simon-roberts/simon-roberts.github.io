Here's the thing nobody tells you about case math: it's rarely what decides the
interview. What it does is take up room. If your head is busy with 4,200 × 0.35,
it isn't free to ask whether that's even the right number to compute, and that
second kind of mistake is the one interviewers remember.

So the goal isn't speed for its own sake. It's making the arithmetic cheap
enough that you can keep thinking while you do it.

## Round first, then tighten

Most candidates try to be exact from the first step. I'd do the opposite: get a
rough answer out loud, then refine it.

Take 4,200 × 0.35. A third of 4,200 is 1,400, so the answer is a bit above that.
Then 4,200 × 0.3 is 1,260, 4,200 × 0.05 is 210, and together that's 1,470.

The interviewer has a usable number in a few seconds and an exact one shortly
after. It works the same way in a client meeting: people want the ballpark
first and the decimals second.

## Learn the conversions that actually come up

Not times tables. These:

- **Percentages as fractions.** 33% is about 1/3, 25% is 1/4, 12.5% is 1/8, and
  17% is about 1/6. Dividing by a whole number beats multiplying by a decimal
  almost every time.
- **Growth rates.** The rule of 72 covers anything a case will throw at you.
  Seven percent a year roughly doubles in a decade.
- **Population anchors.** The U.S. is around 340 million people, Canada around
  41 million, and households are roughly a third of the population. Having three
  or four of these ready saves you from building a market size out of thin air.
- **Per-person scaling.** A dollar per person per year in the U.S. is a market of
  about $340 million. Scaling from that is much faster than building up from
  scratch.

## Keep the zeros separate from the digits

Write 4.2M, not 4,200,000. Do the digits, do the zeros, then put them back
together. Most case math mistakes are order-of-magnitude slips, not digit
slips, and splitting the two steps catches them.

## Say what you're about to do

Narrate before you calculate: "I'll take the fleet size, multiply by annual
maintenance cost per vehicle, then take the share that's warranty-eligible." If
your approach is off, the interviewer can stop you before you spend ninety
seconds on arithmetic heading the wrong way. It also buys you a little thinking
time, which never hurts.

## How to practise

Twenty minutes a day beats two hours on a Sunday.

1. **Timed drills for speed.** Multiplication and percentages, ten minutes.
2. **Estimation for judgment.** Size something and commit to a number before you
   check it: coffee sales on a campus, tires sold in Ontario, elevators in
   Toronto. Ten minutes.
3. **Do it all out loud.** Silent practice builds a skill you can't use in the
   room.

Keep track of your mistakes rather than your times. After a week they usually
fall into one or two patterns, and fixing those does more for your speed than
more drilling ever will.

## The bar is lower than you think

Honestly, it's lower than most candidates assume. Rounding sensibly, checking
whether your answer makes sense and catching your own slips is most of what the
interviewer is watching for. None of it requires you to be quick. It just
requires you to be calm, and practice is what gets you there.
