This is what I'd send a student who asks me how to get into consulting. Most of
the advice out there was written for the U.S., where there are far more firms
and far more seats. Canada runs on the
same playbook, but the timing is earlier and the scale is smaller, and both of
those change how you should play it.

For context, my own route wasn't the tidy one. I came out of UBC Sauder, which
is a semi-target, and did a consulting co-op at KPMG along the way. After I
graduated I spent time on a fellowship in Zanzibar, then in due diligence
research and AI model training, before joining Oliver Wyman in Toronto. So take
this as one consultant's view, from someone who has seen the process from a few
angles.

## The market is small, so act like it

There are only so many strategy offices in Toronto, and fewer again in Calgary,
Montreal and Vancouver. That has three consequences.

**Classes are tiny.** A Canadian office might take a handful of analysts a year.
Being near the top of your cohort is closer to the entry ticket than the thing
that sets you apart.

**Everyone knows everyone.** How you treat a first-round interviewer, a
recruiter or another candidate gets around. That isn't a threat, it's just how
small markets work, and it cuts both ways.

**Conversations matter.** A real conversation with someone two years ahead of
you can be worth more than another practice case. People in this industry are
generally generous with their time if you've done your homework first.

## Start earlier than feels reasonable

Full-time and internship processes at the major firms usually open in the summer
or early fall for the following year, and deadlines come fast. If you're
planning to start practising cases in second term, you've generally missed the
cycle.

Work backwards from the applications:

- **Twelve months out**, figure out whether you actually want this, by talking
  to people who do the job rather than reading about it.
- **Six months out**, start casing. Twenty to thirty live cases with real people
  is a reasonable target, and more if you're at a non-target. The quality of the
  feedback matters more than the count.
- **Three months out**, have your resume, referrals and behavioural stories
  ready to go.

## Target schools, and what to do without one

The firms recruit hardest at a short list, including Western (Ivey) and Queen's
(Smith). UofT (Rotman), McGill (Desautels), UBC (Sauder) and Waterloo (AFM)
round out the semi-targets.

If your school isn't on that list, you're not locked out, but the process
changes. You'll likely apply through general intake, and you'll need someone on
the inside willing to advocate for you. Your resume has to carry more of the
weight, and in practice that means demonstrable work, like competitive co-ops
or internships, more than club leadership.

## What actually gets tested

Both halves of the interview are real, and most candidates over-prepare for the
case.

The case tests structure, comfort with numbers, and whether you can say what
your analysis means for the client's decision. That last part is where most
people come unstuck. They compute everything correctly and then never answer
the question.

The fit interview is underrated, especially here. Offices are small enough that
"would I want this person on a client site for eight weeks?" is a live question.
Have three or four stories covering leadership, conflict and failure, and be
able to tell each one in two minutes without a long windup.

## A few things I wish someone had told me earlier

**Practise with people who'll be blunt.** Nice feedback feels good and teaches
you very little.

**Learn one analytical tool properly.** Excel is table stakes. SQL or Python,
even at a basic level, will set you apart, and it gives you something concrete
to put in your firm CV when you're first up for staffing.

**Get curious about what the office actually works on.** Canadian offices skew
toward financial services, energy, mining, transportation and the public
sector. Genuine interest in that work comes across very differently from
general enthusiasm about "strategy."

**Apply broadly.** The Big Four strategy arms, boutiques and in-house strategy
teams all do interesting work, and people move between them more than you'd
think.

The process is competitive, but it's also predictable. The skills it tests are
the skills you use on the job, and every one of them can be practised. If you're
working through it and want a second opinion, feel free to get in touch.
