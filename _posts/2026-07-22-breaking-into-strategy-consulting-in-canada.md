Most advice about getting into consulting assumes a U.S. market with far more
seats and far more firms. The Canadian version differs mainly in timing and
scale.

## The market is small, so behave accordingly

There are only so many strategy offices in Toronto, and fewer in Calgary,
Montreal and Vancouver. Three consequences follow.

Class sizes are tiny. A Canadian office might take a handful of analysts a year,
so being top of your cohort is closer to the entry requirement than the thing
that sets you apart.

Everyone knows everyone. How you treat a first-round interviewer, a recruiter or
another candidate travels; it's just how small markets work.

Networking pays off. A real conversation with someone two years ahead of you can be
worth more than another practice case.

## Recruiting starts earlier than you think

Full-time and internship processes at the major firms usually open in the summer
or early fall for the following year, and deadlines come quickly. If you wait
until second term to start practising cases, you have generally missed the
cycle.

Work backwards from the applications:

- Twelve months out, work out whether you really want this, by talking to people doing
  the job rather than reading about it.
- Six months out, start casing. Twenty to thirty live cases with real people is a
  reasonable target (and more if you're at a non-target), though the feedback loop matters more than the count.
- Three months out, have the resume, referrals and behavioural stories done.

## Target schools, and what to do without one

The firms recruit heavily at a short list, including Western (Ivey) and Queen's (Smith), while UofT (Rotman),
McGill (Desautels), UBC (Sauder) and Waterloo (AFM) round out the semi-targets. Attending a school off the list
does not lock you out, but the process changes. You'll apply through general
intake, and you'll need someone inside to advocate for you, and your resume will have . In practice that means demonstrable
work (i.e., competitive co-ops or internships) rather than club leadership.

## What actually gets tested

Both halves of the interview are real, and candidates tend to over-prepare for
the case portion.

The case tests structure, quantitative comfort, and whether you can say what
your analysis means for the client's decision. Most people who fail cases fail
on the last part. They compute correctly and then do not answer the question.

The fit interview is underrated here, because offices are small enough that
"would I want this person on a client site for eight weeks" is a live question.
Have three or four stories covering leadership, conflict and failure, and be
able to tell them in two minutes without a windup.

## A few things worth knowing early

Practise with people who will be blunt with you. Encouraging feedback is worse
than none.

Learn one analytical tool properly. Excel is table stakes. SQL and Python even at a basic level, will
separate you from the field and will be useful in your sell-pack/firm CV when you're first up for staffing.

Read about the industries the office actually serves. Canadian offices skew
toward financial services, energy, mining, transportation and the public sector,
and curiosity about the work in front of you reads differently from general
enthusiasm.

Apply broadly. The big four strategy arms, boutiques and in-house strategy teams
do interesting work, and moving between them later is common.

The process is competitive, but it is predictable. The skills tested are
the skills used, and each one of them can be practised.
